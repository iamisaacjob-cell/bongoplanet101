What does “Bongo” mean?

In Swahili, ubongo means brain. Dar es Salaam picked up the nickname Bongo because life in a busy, crowded city asks for quick thinking and a sharp mind.

In the 1990s, young people in Dar began making music in Swahili about their own streets, their own struggles and their own joy. The name came with them, and a whole culture grew around it.

Today, Bongo is the family name for Tanzania’s entertainment: the music, the films, the dance and the humour. This guide introduces five members of that family.

Think of Bongo as a city’s way of talking about itself, set to a beat.
Meet the family

Five sounds, one home

Each one grew from a different corner of Tanzanian life. Read the cards, then pick a favourite.

The pop heartbeat

Bongo Flava

The sound
Swahili vocals over hip-hop, R&B, reggae and dancehall rhythms, with melodies borrowed from taarab and dance bands.
Where it began
Dar es Salaam, in the 1990s.
Why it matters
It is the sound most loved by Tanzanian youth, and it has carried Swahili songs across East Africa.
Start with
“Nataka Kulewa” by Diamond Platnumz
The storytellers

Bongo hip-hop

The sound
Rap in Swahili, with sharp lines about daily life.
Where it began
Hip-hop took root in the late 1980s. Early rappers used English, then moved to Swahili and made the style their own.
Why it matters
It gave Bongo Flava its voice and its rhythm.
Start with
“Ndio Mzee” by Professor Jay
The fastest music in the city

Singeli

The sound
MCs rap at breathless speed over electronic beats of roughly 200 to 300 beats per minute.
Where it began
The working-class neighbourhoods of Tandale and Manzese in Dar es Salaam, around the mid-2000s.
Why it matters
Once dismissed as street music, it is now mainstream at home and loved at festivals abroad.
Start with
“Naona Laaah” by Duke
The song of the coast

Taarab

The sound
Poetic Swahili lyrics over rich orchestral music with roots in Arab and Egyptian traditions.
Where it began
Zanzibar, in the nineteenth century.
Why it matters
It is the music of weddings and celebrations along the Swahili coast, and a parent of later Bongo styles.
Start with
Siti binti Saad and Bi Kidude
Stories on screen

Bongo Movies

The sound and sight
Swahili-language films about love, family and the tests of city life, also called Swahiliwood.
Where it began
Around 2001, as local filmmakers began making their own films.
Why it matters
It put Swahili stories and faces on screens across the region. Actor Steven Kanumba, who died in 2012, is remembered as a pioneer of the industry.
Start with
“Girlfriends” by George Tyson, or “Devil’s Kingdom” starring Steven Kanumba
The story

How we got here

A short journey from the sultan’s palace to the streaming age. Dates marked “around” are the ones sources describe loosely.

  1. 1870 to 1888Sultan Barghash rules Zanzibar. Popular accounts credit him with bringing taarab to the island, after musicians from Egypt came to play at his court.
  2. 1905The Ikhwani Safaa Musical Club is founded in Zanzibar. It still performs today.
  3. 1928Siti binti Saad becomes taarab’s first great star. She sings in Swahili and records in Bombay, taking the music from the palace to ordinary homes.
  4. Late 1980s and early 1990sHip-hop takes root in Dar es Salaam, Arusha and Mwanza. Young Tanzanians begin to rap in Swahili.
  5. Around 1993 and 1994The airwaves open up, and local artists start to be heard on the radio. Bongo Flava begins to spread across the country.
  6. Around 2001Bongo Movies grow into an industry, with local filmmakers telling Swahili stories.
  7. Early 2000sBongo Flava grows from a youth experiment into a national movement.
  8. Around the mid-2000sSingeli is born at block parties in Tandale and Manzese.
  9. 2012Steven Kanumba dies, leaving a deep mark on Bongo Movies.
  10. 2018 onwardSingeli artists take the stage at festivals such as Nyege Nyege in Uganda.
  11. The 2020sBongo Flava is widely described as East Africa’s most dominant sound, and singeli is the newest mainstream style.

Speak the language

Words to know

Eight Swahili words that will help you follow any Bongo conversation.

UbongoBrain. The root of the name “Bongo”.
Kizazi kipya“New generation”. The early name for the music that became Bongo Flava.
UswahiliniThe lively, working-class neighbourhoods where much of Bongo began.
KigodoroA neighbourhood block party, where singeli was born.
NgomaDrum, and the music and dance that go with it.
DansiDance-band music, an older sound that shaped Bongo Flava.
MtaaA street or neighbourhood. “Mtaani” means in the neighbourhood.
MsaniiAn artist, whether a singer, actor or performer.
